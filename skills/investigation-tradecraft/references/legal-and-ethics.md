# Legal and ethical practice

Where investigations most often go wrong legally and ethically, drawn from private-investigator practice, professional codes, and the scandals that shaped them. This is not legal advice. Statutes and rules differ by jurisdiction, and the US and UK examples below are illustrations. Decisions in this area belong to the human, often with counsel.

## Who is asking, and why

Locating people is the work most easily turned into harm. In 1989, a private investigator obtained actress Rebecca Schaeffer's address from California motor-vehicle records, and the stalker who hired him used it to kill her. That case led to the US Driver's Privacy Protection Act. In 1999, the data broker Docusearch sold Amy Boyer's workplace address to the man who killed her. *Remsburg v. Docusearch* (New Hampshire, 2003) is reported to have held that investigators owe a duty when a disclosure could foreseeably lead to criminal harm. UK domestic-abuse charities report that abusers still hire investigators to trace partners who have fled.

Before a locate, it helps to know:
- who the requester is, and their relationship to the subject;
- why they need a current address, and whether legal process stands behind the request;
- whether there is a protective order, a history of threats, or a plan to confront the subject.

Some requests are red flags:
- an estranged partner;
- urgency with no legal process behind it;
- a refusal to explain the purpose;
- a request that grows from a background check into ongoing monitoring.

Someone who fled may be described in the brief as "a debtor" or "a missing relative".

Often the need can be met without releasing an address: confirming the person was found, serving papers through a process server or the court, or passing a message on. When the stated purpose doesn't fit the request, that is a decision for the human, not something to finish quietly.

Licensing doesn't guarantee vetting. The UK, for example, legislated for PI licensing in 2001 and announced it in 2013, but reporting suggests it never came into force.

## Each data source has permitted uses

Reliability and permissibility are separate grades. Data from regulated sources, and broker products built on them, can usually be used only for listed purposes. Regulated sources include motor-vehicle, credit, telecom and financial records. Some US examples:

- **DPPA (18 U.S.C. §2721).** It gives licensed investigators no purpose of their own: they can use motor-vehicle data only for purposes the Act already allows. A recipient who resells or rediscloses the data must keep records for five years of each onward recipient and its permitted purpose.
- **FCRA (15 U.S.C. §1681a).** It can turn an investigator's report into a "consumer report", with all the obligations that brings, for example interviews with neighbours used in a hiring decision. Workplace-misconduct investigations have a conditional carve-out.
- **Postal forwarding data** is, by secondary accounts, limited to purposes such as service of process.

In the UK, section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 makes it an offence to obtain, disclose or retain personal data without the controller's consent, knowingly or recklessly. Under UK GDPR, covert processing needs a legitimate-interests assessment covering purpose, necessity and balance. The ICO approved the Association of British Investigators' code of conduct in 2024, which applies this to tracing, surveillance and social-media monitoring.

The practical habit is to write down the lawful purpose before using a regulated source. In GDPR-style regimes, also note why the processing is necessary and how it balances against the subject's rights. If no purpose can be written down, that is usually the answer.

## Personas, pretexts and deception

A research account used to read public pages is an OpSec measure. A persona used to gain access is deception, and some kinds are crimes in themselves:
- a friend or follow request;
- joining a closed group;
- a pretext call;
- posing as the account holder.

US examples:

- **Pretexting for financial records.** Lying to get a financial institution's customer data is a federal offence (GLBA, 15 U.S.C. §6821), and so is asking someone else to do it.
- **Pretexting for phone records** is covered by 18 U.S.C. §1039, with sentences of up to ten years.
- **Lawyers and their agents.** Bar ethics opinions allow lawyers to view public social-media profiles. They do not allow friending a represented party, directly or through an agent, or hiding the lawyer's connection when friending a witness. Where lawyers are involved, an agent's sock puppet is treated as the lawyer's own.

Black Cube's operatives used false identities to approach Rose McGowan and journalists. Separately, the firm's staff were convicted in Romania over an operation targeting the anti-corruption prosecutor.

Using a persona to gain access is the human's decision, made with counsel where relevant. The record should show which persona was used and what it said.

## Bought results don't launder the method

When a vendor, a contractor or the client supplies phone, bank, police-database or carrier records, the important question is how they were obtained. Records that can normally only come from the account holder, from legal process or from an insider usually mean something went wrong.

- **HP, 2006.** A boardroom leak investigation passed through subcontractors who pretexted for board members' and journalists' phone records. Outside counsel had called the work lawful. HP paid $14.5 million, and its chair and general counsel resigned.
- **Pellicano.** Anthony Pellicano paid police officers to run law-enforcement database checks and wiretapped targets. He was convicted of racketeering and wiretapping in 2008.
- **Operation Motorman.** The UK Information Commissioner's investigation found one investigator's files listed hundreds of journalists who had requested "blagged" personal data.

Material like this is best treated as tainted until its route is known, and its origin recorded. A lawyer's reassurance doesn't change what was actually done.

## Recording, interception and tracking

- **Recording consent varies by place.** About a dozen US states require every party's consent to record a conversation, and some treat phone calls and in-person conversations differently. Where the parties are in different places, the stricter rule may govern.
- **Accessing someone's voicemail or message store is interception, not research**, even with a default PIN. A private investigator and a journalist at the News of the World pleaded guilty in 2006 to intercepting voicemail, and were jailed in 2007.
- **GPS trackers** are restricted in many places.

When recording or tracking is on the table, it helps to name which rules might apply and leave the decision with the human.

## Notes, privilege and discoverability

- **Logs and drafts can be produced in litigation.** That includes notes, logs, chat transcripts and drafts, and the subject or opposing counsel may read them.
- **Work product protection.** In the US, material prepared in anticipation of litigation by a party's representative is protected unless the other side shows substantial need (FRCP 26(b)(3)). Mental impressions get stronger protection.
- **Testifying narrows protection.** It can be waived when the investigator testifies (*US v. Nobles*), and FRE 612 lets the other side inspect writings used to refresh a witness's memory.
- **Write the log as if opposing counsel will read it.** That protects both the case and the people named in it: observations first, then labelled inferences, with no speculation about people.
- **Record the engagement at the start.** Who commissioned the work, and whether it came through counsel, affects what can be withheld later.

## Independence from the client

Findings answer to the evidence, not to whoever is paying. In England and Wales, an expert's duty to the court overrides any obligation to the party who instructs or pays them (CPR 35.3). Signs that work is drifting into advocacy:
- fees tied to an outcome;
- a client asking for "something on" a person;
- requests to drop exculpatory material.

When one of these signs appears, it's worth naming it to the human and tying the work back to the decision it serves. Personal material wanted as leverage rather than for that decision (health, family, sexuality, old debts) is out of scope, and pressing someone with it can amount to coercion or blackmail.

Anything that may reach a court is worth writing as if under oath:
- what was done, seen and inferred;
- the limits of the work;
- the facts that cut the other way.

Reporting a finding the client didn't want is part of the job.

## Conflicts of interest

Before starting, it helps to check whether the subject, the adverse party or a likely witness is, or was, a client or connected to a related matter. If so, the human decides whether to proceed. Material from one engagement isn't reused in another without authority.

## Who the report is for

Many legal and insurance clients want a fact report: what was found, where, when, and from which record, with conclusions left to them. For that audience, likelihood judgments are better placed in a separate, clearly labelled section, or left out. Surveillance reports in particular separate:
- what was observed;
- what wasn't observed;
- what couldn't be determined.

Words about character or state of mind ("reformed", "evasive", "suspicious") are opinions, even when they feel like description.

## Cases and lessons

| Case | What happened | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rebecca Schaeffer (1989)** | A PI obtained her address from motor-vehicle records for the stalker who killed her | Finding someone and releasing their address are separate decisions; the request is part of the risk |
| **Amy Boyer / Docusearch (1999)** | A broker sold her workplace address to her killer | Investigators can owe a duty to the subject when a disclosure foreseeably enables harm |
| **HP pretexting (2006)** | Subcontractors pretexted for board members' and journalists' phone records | Outsourcing doesn't launder the method; a legal opinion doesn't make it lawful |
| **Pellicano (2008 conviction)** | Paid police for database checks and wiretapped targets | Records from improper access taint everything built on them |
| **Operation Motorman / News of the World** | Blagged personal data and voicemail interception at scale | Interception and blagging are crimes, not research shortcuts |
| **Black Cube** | False identities used to approach an accuser and journalists; staff convicted in Romania | Deception to gain access has legal and reputational consequences far beyond the case |
